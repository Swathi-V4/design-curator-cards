Bauhaus

1919–1933

Bauhaus was founded in 1919 in Weimar, Germany by architect Walter Gropius. The school sought to unify art, craft, and technology into a single design philosophy. Rather than separating fine art from industrial production, Bauhaus encouraged collaboration between artists, architects, and designers. Its visual language emphasized geometric forms, clean lines, sans-serif typography, and minimal ornamentation. The movement promoted functionalism — the idea that form should follow function — and aimed to design objects and buildings that were both beautiful and practical. Bauhaus had a profound influence on architecture, graphic design, furniture design, and modernist visual culture worldwide, shaping the development of modern design in the twentieth century.

Not to be confused with generic minimalism, because Bauhaus was rooted in social reform and industrial production rather than purely aesthetic simplicity.

Swiss Style (International Typographic Style)

1950s–1970s

Swiss Style, also known as the International Typographic Style, emerged in Switzerland in the 1950s. It emphasized clarity, objectivity, and structured visual communication. Designers used grid systems to organize content with precision and balance. Sans-serif typefaces such as Helvetica became central to the movement’s identity. Swiss Style prioritized readability and neutrality, removing unnecessary decoration in favor of clean layouts and strong typographic hierarchy. It became highly influential in corporate branding, editorial design, and public signage, shaping modern graphic design standards worldwide.

Not to be confused with Bauhaus, because Swiss Style focused primarily on graphic design and typography rather than architectural and industrial experimentation.

New Wave

1970s–1980s

New Wave design emerged in the 1970s as a reaction against the rigid grid systems and neutrality of Swiss Style. Influenced by punk culture, postmodern theory, and experimental typography, New Wave designers embraced asymmetry, layered text, vibrant colors, and expressive visual forms. Rather than prioritizing strict readability and objectivity, New Wave explored emotion, movement, and visual disruption. Designers such as Wolfgang Weingart challenged traditional typographic rules, introducing broken grids and dynamic compositions. The movement expanded the boundaries of graphic design by encouraging personal expression and conceptual experimentation.
